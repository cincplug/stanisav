## Who is Stanisav?

Stanisav is an animated 3D character who physically visualizes the linguistic properties of languages.

## How does he do it?

Every language is different. They can be isolating, agglutinative, fusional or polysynthetic. Some have declension, others have evidentiality or verb aspect, different number of phonemes, genders, or flexibility of the word order. Some are tonal, some have pitch accent, and all kinds of other properties, just like some dogs have bigger muzzle and some cats have longer tail.

These properties are visualised by Stanisav by mapping them to physical properties of his face.

- Morphology determines the shape of his face
- Evidentiality influences how big his eyes are
- Verb aspects decides on width of his eyelids
- Word order is reflected by parts of his nose
- Word order flexibility decides how much he jumps around
- The more phonemes, the more teeth
- The more genders, the more eyebrows
- The more declension cases, the more tufts in his moustache

## Where does he live?

He lives on a planet made of languages instead of countries. No ground, no oceans, no borders. Languages are distributed evenly, regardless of their speakers count. They have generative colors, independent of national flags. You can sort them and filter them by various linguistic criteria.

Click on any language: Stanisav will get its facial features, you will hear its sample, by its native speaker and also by Stanisav's designer. Stanisav will animate following the audio visualisation, and if language has more flexible word order, Stanisav will also jump around a little.

## What can you do with Stanisav?

I exposed most of the app settings, click around, you can modify practically anything. It is for everyone, especially for designers, linguists, musicians, and generally curious people

### If you are interested in design

Expand the menu, click on Controls tab, there you have most of his visual controls, you can customize them drastically:

- Stanisav's appearance
- ways how he visualises the sound of language samples
- ways how languages are distributed accross their "Earth"
- colors, shader materials, motion and all

If things get out of hand while customizing, good old "Reset to defaults" button is at the bottom

### If you are interested in music

Here you have all 232 languages spoken by myself. It might sound like I am singing, but I am not. I'm just talking, like the people in original samples. It might sound like singing because every language has its melody. In tonal languages, the pitch even determines the meaning, and in pitch-accent langauges it is similar.

To emphasise the musical aspect of languages, I also added acoustic bass guitar to my samples. In most cases, it follows the speech. But in some cases, I used the opportunity to play my favourite classic composers. If you are that kind of a nerd, find how many samples are borrowed from Beethoven, Brahms, and especially Dvořák.

Original language samples are gatered from all kinds of YouTube videos: language learning, news, hand crafts, cooking. For now, you can also hear original samples after my versions. As soon as any of source video authors starts complaining, which is statistically bound to happen, I'll have to remove the originals. In any case, there is always a link to the source video in the bottom bar.

### If you are interested in linguistics or if you are a teacher

- use `Sort by` dropdown to sort languages according to different criteria
- use `App language` dropdown to see the entire app in a different language
- use `Filters` tab to limit the choice of languages to particular criteria, very useful if 232 languages comes overwhelming
- use `Label content` dropdown to show languages by their native names, regardless of chosen app language
- or, if you are a nerd like me, use it to show them by their three-letter ISO codes. It's totally cryptic but beautiful
- for even bigger nerds, there is Kavski, more about Kavski in the following section

## What is Kavski?

Kavski is one of last additions to the app, it is a different model of naming languages.

Generally the languages are named after nations speaking them. That's exactly the aspect that I want to avoid. Stanisav is made to be about languages in their essence, devoid of their national origin. As I already said, that's also why I use generative color scale instead of what most people would expect: national flags, coats of arms or football dress colors.

But what kind of names should I give to languages? Well I happen to know one model, from my native language, former Serbo-Croatian, nowadays Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian, Montenegrin. Before it got split across national boundaries, it had three dialects: Shtokavian, Kajkavian, and Chakavian. These names were not national, but obtained from how you say the word "what" in each of those dialects.

So, in Stanisav I got an idea to just expand that model to all languages. For example:

- English => what => Whatkavian
- German => was => Waskavian
- Italian => cosa => Cosakavian

At first, it surely looks mad. But as in Shakespeare: _Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t._

Try it out, and you'll see that it's a great model, I do hope it will eventually prevail. It naturally puts related languages close to each other in alphabetical order, and more importantly it lets you learn one of the most important word in all langauges.

But I am not that mad to make it default in the app, and currently it's in just a few locale's. If you want to see Kavski names, choose it from Label content dropdown in the menu. If they aren't yet present in your language, they will simply default to their English version.

Yes, you will notice that Spanish and Portugese both became Quekavian, while Czech and Polish both became Cokavian, and there are whole groups with same name. But I didn't say that this model is perfect, I only said that it's great.

## Is this also suitable for my kids?

In my opinion, yes. Stanisav is a cartoon character after all, he is meant to look funny and friendly. Your kids, or your pupils if you are a teacher, will get familiar with an unusual graphic concept and they'll at least hear many foreign languages before they get to develop repulsion to some of them.
